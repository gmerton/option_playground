## Raw notes

- [00:00] Frame: "most traders view a chart setup as binary, you either take the trade or you
  don't." He grades A–D instead, and **the grade determines size**. One system, applied to every
  trade.
- [00:47–00:58] Poker analogy runs through the whole video: different hands are different quality;
  a grade is not a prediction, it's an EV ranking.
- [01:09] A–D scale, **plus/minus refinements** for the nuances in between.

### The four grades — per TRADE, assigned BEFORE entry, mapped to SIZE

| Grade | Meaning | Size |
|---|---|---|
| **D** [01:19–01:41] | No trade. "Literally, 99.9% of charts are D grade or worse." Equivalent to the hands you fold. "A D means you pass on it. Doesn't matter how much you want it, doesn't matter that you've been bored all day." | zero |
| **C** [01:52–02:22] | "Small capital or a feeler trade… equivalent to limping into the pot." A real setup where "most of the stars are just not aligning" — maybe two boxes checked, maybe the market isn't cooperating. "You're basically paying a small fee to stay engaged and to be positioned in case the setup improves." Must still be "at least marginally break even expected value or better." | a fraction of normal |
| **B** [02:22–02:41] | "Your bread and butter. Normal everyday trade. Your standard size. Most of your trades… should be B trades." Checks the boxes, nothing exceptional, positive EV, "you take [it] hundreds of times per year, and your edge plays out through repetition. B trades are boring, and boring is good." | standard |
| **A** [02:41–03:12] | "Exceptional. This is the one. Everything lines up. The chart, the catalyst, the market, the tape." Best trade of the day or the handful of best of the month. "This is where you size up." | up |

- [03:01] "Most traders' P&L, if you actually break it down, comes from a small number of these
  trades. The A system is about recognizing those trades **before** they play out, not after."
- [03:12–03:43] ⚠ **He disclaims all objectivity**: "These grades and the plusses and minuses, it's
  all subjective, completely subjective. My A is not your A trade… There is no formal definition."
  You define grades from "your strategy, your risk tolerance, and your historical data."
- [03:43] "The point is not to copy my rubric. The point is **to have a rubric**" — to force the
  question "how good is this trade?" before every entry.
- [04:04–04:34] ⭐ The generalization: grading an essay. "It's not one thing. It's how the series of
  qualities come together. Each category gets scored and the total determines the grade. A paper
  can have beautiful writing and a garbage argument and land a C." → **a setup is a weighted
  scorecard over variables, not a binary pattern match.**
- [04:34–05:04] ⚠ Explicit course pitch (theonelanceb.com), plus a follow-count guilt bit.

### ⭐ One rubric per setup

- [05:04–05:36] "You cannot grade a capitulation trade with a breakout rubric. That's like grading
  a math test with a history essay rubric. The variables that make a panic sell-off an A+ trade are
  almost the opposite of the variables that make a breakout an A+ trade. **So you need a separate
  scorecard for every setup in your playbook.**"

**Capitulation rubric (5 variables)** [05:36–07:48]:
1. **Rate of change** — "a stock that grinds down 3% over a day is not capitulation. That's just a
   down day." Wants the waterfall. Faster/more extreme → more forced selling → more elastic snapback.
2. ⭐ **How boring the stock is** — "a sleepy, nobody cares, low beta name that suddenly drops 15%
   in a day. That's unusual… way more likely to be an overreaction. But a volatile momentum name
   that swings 15% every other Tuesday, a big drop there is just, well, a Tuesday." **Boring stock,
   violent move, that's the combo.** Example: his STRC long ("boring preferred security, large
   sharp move").
3. **Daily chart** — falling into multi-year support, or a flush inside a longer-term uptrend.
   "Catching a falling knife in a chart that's been dying for 6 months, that's how you donate money."
4. **Intraday chart** — "because I'm generally an intraday trader… this accounts for far more
   points than anything else on the rubric." Everything else would have to line up perfectly to
   override it.
5. **News** — wants **no news**, or old news overblown relative to the move. "A 20% drop because
   earnings were catastrophic… that's not capitulation. That's just repricing the news."
   → Worked example [07:48]: boring utility-type name, −12% in two hours, no news, into long-term
   support, climactic tape = **A, size up**.

**Breakout rubric (5 variables)** [07:59–09:42]:
1. **Quality of the consolidation** — how long and how tight. "Long, tight, clean consolidation
   grades high… short, loose, messy consolidation grades low."
2. **Volume** — "confirmation that everyone else was also paying attention to this level."
3. **In-play, ideally on news** — leader in a hot sector with a real narrative, not "some random
   laggard drifting into a level."
4. ⚠ **Overall market conditions** — barely matters for capitulation (idiosyncratic dislocations)
   but dominant for breakouts: "**this one variable can single-handedly downgrade an A setup to a C
   or worse.**" "If the indices are getting sold, most breakouts are traps."
5. **Quality of the daily chart / MTF alignment** — "really great daily charts pull in everyone."

### Implementation — three steps [09:42–10:30]

1. **Write the rubrics down, physically.** Per setup, "list the four to six variables that matter
   the most. **If you can't list them, you don't have a clearly defined setup. You're just trading
   feelings.**"
2. **Grade every trade BEFORE you enter it** and write the grade in the journal, "not after. This
   helps hold you accountable and avoid hindsight bias."
3. ⭐ **After a few months, compare your grades to your results. "Are your A trades actually
   outperforming your B trades? If yes, your rubric is real and you should be sizing more
   aggressively on your A trades. If no, your criteria are wrong and now you know exactly what to
   fix." "The grades turn your journal into actual data."**

## Claims to verify

- [ ] ⭐ **"Literally, 99.9% of charts are D grade or worse" [01:30].** Cheap check against our own
      funnel: universe ≈1,700 names × sessions vs the layer-2 in-play count (2,439 name-days in the
      entry study). If our gates pass ~1% of name-days we are calibrated to him; if we pass 10% we
      are running a much looser D line than he claims to, which bears directly on
      `feedback_precision_over_recall`.
- [ ] ⭐ **"Boring stock, violent move" [06:15].** The one genuinely testable mechanic in the video,
      on daily bars already on disk: rank selloffs by **drop% ÷ the name's own trailing ADR** and
      cross it with **low prior ADR** (the "boring" leg). Prediction: reversion is concentrated
      where prior ADR is low and the shock/ADR ratio is extreme. This is a real refinement of
      `project_crash_leader_study`, which concluded buying deep selloffs is a *regime* bet, not
      selection — his claim is that selection works **once you normalize the move by the name's own
      normal range**, which that study did not do. Same normalization as the in-play gate in
      [`in-play-stocks.md`](../../../principles/in-play-stocks.md).
- [ ] **No-news veto on fades [07:25].** Now the **fourth** independent statement of this rule in
      the repo (here, `remaining-five.md`, `capitulation-and-trade-writeups.md`, Carter's veto
      list). Still untested. The gap study never conditioned on catalyst at all.
- [ ] ⚠ **"Market conditions can single-handedly downgrade an A breakout to a C or worse" [09:11].**
      **Contradicted by two repo studies.** `project_august_2026_retrospective`: the breadth gate
      was nil and weak breadth was a mild BUY, not a sell, 2019–2026.
      `project_breakout_regime_feedback`: the paying months cannot be forecast from SPY state or
      anything else, and the only regime lever that worked was fixed small sizing rather than a
      switch. Directly testable on the 2,439 layer-2 name-days: does an SPY-trend gate improve
      breakout R? The repo's answer so far is no.
- [ ] **"Most traders' P&L comes from a small number of these trades" [03:01].** Agrees with the
      repo: `project_breakout_regime_feedback` (top 10% of months = 68% of positive R) and his own
      [`risk-management-15-lessons.md`](../../../principles/risk-management-15-lessons.md)
      (80% of profits from 1–5% of trades). Consistent, and consistent with
      `project_vrp_straddle_reconcile` (99.9% of straddle P&L from the top 0.1% of trades) —
      though that one is a warning about fragility, not a licence to size up.
- [ ] **"All the traders at SMB Capital live and die by this framework" [10:30].** Appeal to

## Reactions / conflicts

### Per-trade or per-day? — settles the question

**Per TRADE, assigned BEFORE entry, and its only output is SIZE.** That makes it a different object
from the daily report card in `vKy6Q9hwon4` (per day, after the close, output = tomorrow's focus).
Same letters, different systems. In the repo those two objects are already correctly split:
`src/lib/alerts/grading.py` (per-setup A/B/C/F) and `run_journal_grades.py` (per-day process card).
The conflation lives only in the secondhand note.

### Rubric vs `src/lib/alerts/grading.py` — where we agree, and where we are ahead

**Agreement, and it is structural.** His [04:04] essay-grading argument — a setup is a *weighted
score over several variables*, and the total sets the grade — is the same conclusion the repo
reached independently in `feedback_breakout_scorecard` ("scorecard, not binary, for setup
qualification: value + threshold + tier per gate"). Two sources, same conclusion, arrived at
differently. His step 3 [10:10] is also **exactly** the loop `run_alert_study.py --report grades`
runs.

**Where the repo is ahead of him, concretely.** His validation step says: compare your A trades to
your B trades; if A doesn't win, your criteria are wrong. The repo ran that loop on 19,456 alerts
over 153 sessions — and the v1→v2 rubric revision killed the A grade because ORB9≥10:00 was +0.70R
on the curated universe but **−0.39R (83% stopped) on a 39-name no-hindsight control set**. In other
words the grade was scoring the *universe*, not the *setup*. **He never mentions a control.** Under
his instructions that rubric would have passed and been sized up on. This is the single most
important delta in the video, and it runs in our favour.

**Where our rubric is thinner than his.** Ours is time-of-day and day-state only (LONG/SHORT
in-play gate, 09:41–12:00 window, the +3% vs SPY short veto). His two sample rubrics carry
*structural* variables ours deliberately dropped or never had: consolidation length/tightness,
volume confirmation, catalyst freshness, MTF alignment. Note that several of those **were tested and
dropped for cause** (SPY vs VWAP, relative strength vs SPY or group, stop-in-noise, still-below-
9-EMA: "tested, no stable signal"), so this is not an oversight — but his criteria 1, 2 and 5 for
breakouts have *not* been tested in that harness and are the natural v3 candidates.

### ⚠ Three collisions with house rules and tested repo results

1. **"Size up on A" vs the size-lever study.** `project_size_lever_study` (2026-09-18) tested
   exactly this and found **the lever is exclusion, not a risk spread**: A+B only = +0.29R OOS,
   while a 10× risk spread across grades gave +0.08R *with more drawdown*, and the stop-distance
   cell failed as a grade outright. His D-is-a-fold rule is therefore **confirmed**; his
   A-gets-size rule is **not supported on our data**. Same verdict lands on
   [`risk-framework-longform.md`](../../../principles/risk-framework-longform.md) ($10k B →
   $100k A). Adopt the exclusion half, hold the sizing half at low conviction.
2. **"C = feeler trade to stay engaged" [01:52] is the direct opposite of two house rules.**
   `feedback_precision_over_recall` ("rather miss winners than admit losers") and the size-lever
   finding that exclusion is the whole edge both say a marginal trade taken to stay warm is the
   trade to drop. It is also the exact behaviour our own process grade penalises twice — through
   the fills cap and the same-day round-trip component — and the behaviour the August lens costed
   at −$7.9k. ⚠ **Do not import the C tier.** His justification ("paying a small fee to stay
   engaged") is a prop-desk seat-time argument, not an EV argument, and he half-admits it by
   requiring C to be "at least marginally break even."
3. ⚠ **His validation loop [10:10] violates `feedback_gabes_trades_are_not_evidence`.** "Compare
   your grades to your results" makes *your own trade outcomes* the evidence base for whether a
   setup rubric is real. House rule: Gabe's log is conformance / execution / cost realism only,
   never setup selection. The adoptable form is: **grade pre-entry, log the grade, but validate the
   rubric on the replay population** (`run_alert_study.py`), not on the book. His version is how a
   discretionary trader with 10,000 trades a year can do it; at our trade count it would be noise
   even if the house rule allowed it.

### Smaller notes

- "Grade before entry, never after" [10:04] is worth adopting verbatim and is nearly free: the
  alert engine already grades at fire time, so the missing piece is only that a *discretionary*
  entry with no alert behind it currently gets its grade reconstructed after the fact by
  `lib/journal/entry_grades.py`. Pre-committing the grade would remove that reconstruction.
- "One rubric per setup" [05:15] is a real gap: the repo runs **one** rubric across every alert
  kind, with the short side handled by extra vetoes rather than its own scorecard. His argument
  that the capitulation variables are "almost the opposite" of the breakout variables is a fair
  criticism of that design, and it matches `feedback_one_rubric_alerts_and_journal`'s intent
  (one rubric shared by alerts *and* journal) being about *consumers*, not *setups*.
- ⚠ Every illustration is a winner described after the fact (STRC, the utility name), with zero
  base rates and no numbers anywhere in 11 minutes.
