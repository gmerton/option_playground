## Risk & position sizing

- **Risk a small fixed fraction per trade — ~0.3% (was ~0.5%, dropping as account and
  trade-count grew); only a once-in-a-decade parabolic with a very tight stop earns more.**
  The bedrock number, repeated all year (sometimes phrased 0.2–0.5%). A hard-capped tiny risk
  lets you take dozens of attempts cheaply and survive a decade without going bankrupt; risk
  varies only *slightly* with setup quality (0.2% B-setup vs 0.25% baseline) — never half- or
  double-size on conviction alone. The interview frames the baseline as ~0.5% on average, sizing
  up (toward 6–7% position, not risk) only on high conviction or with a profit cushion. The tiny
  number is matched to the low win rate of a tight-stop style (a wider-stop, higher-win-rate
  approach can justify more risk per trade), and the 2026 cut from 0.5% to 0.3% is compensated
  by a higher number of entries.

- **Tight stops, not big size, are the lever — position size = (account × risk%) ÷ stop%.**
  Because risk is fixed tiny, a tight ~1–2.5% stop (usually LOD, sometimes the bar low or just
  below anchored VWAP) lets you put on a *large* 20–30% position for the same small dollar risk;
  he tightened stops over years (5–8% → ~1–1.5%) and performance lifted off as he did. The
  interview makes the tradeoff explicit: tightening a stop from 3% to 1.5% can pay back ~8 losing
  trades on a 25% winner (20–25R on bigger ones). Always define and place the stop *before*
  entering — every loss known and accepted. Pre-compute size before the open (shares = account
  risk ÷ stop distance); size shorts exactly the same way; and always hunt for the entry tactic
  that tightens the stop — a tighter, logical stop is what maximizes risk/reward per trade.

- **Risk management is the holy grail — define and accept the loss before entering; it never
  changes even as entry tactics do.** Entry style (breakouts vs pullbacks) rotates with the
  market; capping the downside and keeping losses small is the one constant that keeps you in
  the game long enough to learn. Place the stop before entry *even when bending other rules* —
  the stop order goes in immediately after the fill (never sit in a position without a working
  stop), it marks the level that proves the trade wrong, and it never gets widened after entry;
  trying to outsmart stops occasionally helps but loses over time.

- **Never increase risk in a drawdown — reduce size; sizing up to win it back is a death
  spiral.** His most painful lessons (a 0.5% risk turned into a 35% drawdown; 2024 mistakes)
  all came from raising risk while losing. In a drawdown either you're trading badly or the
  market doesn't suit your style — smaller size is the answer to both. The urge to size up and
  win it back "as fast as possible" (his own instinct 2–3 years ago) is the first step of the
  vicious cycle — even after a single day's string of stop-outs, keep size flat and don't keep
  shorting every bounce regardless of the risk or the market.

- **Size down on illiquid / small-micro-cap / high-gap-risk names — and never average down.**
  Wide-spread sub-$3 names, ADRs, and sub-$2B caps can gap 30%+ through your stop; cut size or
  avoid them. Never add to a loser; cut losses fast, don't become a bag holder — once price is
  already below your stop, waiting and hoping generally means a larger loss; take the exit.

- **Don't cap total margin/exposure explicitly — cap risk *per trade* and let exposure build
  naturally as positions work and you de-risk them.** Size off realized equity, not unrealized
  profits: when the account doubles, count only ~half the unrealized gains and step up size
  slowly. Raising risk on the way up but failing to size down on the way down was his biggest
  past mistake; slow scaling makes it easier to scale back. The interview pegs the natural ceiling
  at ~280% long, reached only because positions are working. Leverage doesn't change per-trade
  risk when tight stops define it — it just lets you hold many small-risk positions at once and
  finance new trades with open profits when the market is paying.

- **Aggression must be earned, twice over: a large profit cushion to absorb the risk AND a proven
  ability to throttle down in losing streaks — without both, the same aggressive flip is just
  overtrading toward a large drawdown.** He can flip from heavily long at one open to 6–7 short
  positions the next day only because a triple-digit YTD cushion absorbs the risk ("if I'm not up
  triple digits right now, probably I'm not opening six or seven short positions in one day") and
  he's gotten better at cutting size when losing. Aggressive sizing is only an edge when done
  correctly — in hindsight the winners look like front-running, but the same behavior failing
  produces a large drawdown; reward always comes from risk, and the job is maximizing return
  while actively managing the drawdown. "You don't have to trade like this."

- **Under-size new positions when already loaded / the market is stretched.** Many working
  positions means the market is likely extended, so new entries carry a lower win rate; make
  new-trade size similar to or smaller than existing ones to control drawdown and protect
  mental capital for holding the winners you already have. Stop adding entirely once total open
  risk is too heavy (he skipped Marvell after five new buys), and cap same-sector exposure —
  skip an otherwise-good setup when you already hold several names in that sector on the same
  side.

- **Anticipating a breakdown/breakout before confirmation = take less risk (e.g. half size).**
  Aggressive early entries before the chart confirms are lower-probability, so cut the

  tighter risk — offset it with tiny risk (~0.1–0.15%) and a willingness to re-enter.

## Market posture & regime

- **Index corrections start from an EXTENSION or a FAILED BREAKOUT, almost never from a flat
  base — and the market never crashes in a day.** 2007, the 2018 trade-war break, and the sharp
  2024 pullback all began from one of those two structures; a sideways range with neither
  present is too early to call a top. A real downtrend announces itself first: leader
  breakdowns, failed breakouts, volume and volatility expansion. Wait for the danger signs
  instead of front-running a top call — top-ticking to stand out from the crowd isn't smart, and
  the market can run far beyond what you can imagine.

- **The checklist for turning bullish again after de-risking: breadth, follow-through, your own
  trade feedback, and the index reclaiming its declining 9 EMA.** (1) More names showing
  strength and potential leadership; (2) breakouts/reversals that follow through instead of
  failing instantly; (3) new longs giving positive P&L feedback; (4) at minimum, QQQ back above
  the declining 9. Until those appear, stay defensive. *(thin)*

- **The general market is the most important filter; bias bullish in an uptrend — better too
  bullish than too bearish, and let the market dictate the setup.** Environment contributes more
  than half of any trade's success; in a strong uptrend be aggressive and buy as much as you can,
  because you don't know how long it lasts and top-ticking doesn't pay. Trade more
  pullbacks/breakouts/EPs/parabolics depending on what the current tape rewards — the tight-stop,
  low-win-rate style stays constant, only the entry tactic adapts.
