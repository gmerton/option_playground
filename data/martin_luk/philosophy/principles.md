## Risk & position sizing

- **Tight stops, not big size, are the lever — position size = (account × risk%) ÷ stop%.**
  Because risk is fixed tiny, a tight ~1–2.5% stop (usually LOD, sometimes the bar low or just
  below anchored VWAP) lets you put on a *large* 20–30% position for the same small dollar risk;
  he tightened stops over years (5–8% → ~1–1.5%) and performance lifted off as he did. The
  interview makes the tradeoff explicit: tightening a stop from 3% to 1.5% can pay back ~8 losing
  trades on a 25% winner (20–25R on bigger ones). Always define and place the stop *before*
  entering — every loss known and accepted. Pre-compute size before the open (shares = account
  risk ÷ stop distance); size shorts exactly the same way; risk is derived from stop distance, not
  a stock's ADR or beta, so two names with very different volatility (20% ADR vs 5% ADR) can carry
  the same dollar risk. Always hunt for the entry tactic that tightens the stop — his blunt
  summary of the whole style is "focus on getting entries with the tightest possible risk right
  before a range expansion." A tight, unambiguous stop can even be the PRIMARY REASON to take a
  mediocre-looking trade: shorting AMD in July 2026 despite an extended, lower-win-rate entry —
  "my reason AMD today will be a good short is plainly because there's a tight stop... I can get a
  pretty high potential risk/reward," with the stop ~1% away at a level (high of day = the 9 EMA)
  where "you can easily know you're wrong." When no structure exists to anchor a stop (a fast,
  gappy first-minute candle), fall back to a fixed percentage stop (~1.5–2.5%) rather than chase a
  name already extended past its breakout level, accepting the lower win rate; an entry generally
  isn't worth taking at all once the required stop passes ~2%, and if a setup genuinely needs a
  wider stop, cut position size to keep dollar risk normal rather than widen the risk budget.
  Tightening a stop doesn't mechanically change expectancy, but the larger R-multiple it buys
  tends to outweigh the lower win rate because good trades rarely pull back more than ~1–1.5%
  before continuing his way — and as the account grows, keep sizing down and tightening further
  (core positions sometimes stopped as tight as ~0.5% price / ~0.15% account risk) purely to keep
  slippage manageable at scale.

- **Risk a small fixed fraction per trade — ~0.3% (was ~0.5%, dropping as account and
  trade-count grew); only a once-in-a-decade parabolic with a very tight stop earns more.**
  The bedrock number, repeated all year (sometimes phrased 0.2–0.5%). A hard-capped tiny risk
  lets you take dozens of attempts cheaply and survive a decade without going bankrupt; risk
  varies only *slightly* with setup quality (0.2% B-setup vs 0.25% baseline) — never half- or
  double-size on conviction alone. The interview frames the baseline as ~0.5% on average, sizing
  up (toward 6–7% position, not risk) only on high conviction or with a profit cushion. The tiny
  number is matched to the low win rate of a tight-stop style (a wider-stop, higher-win-rate

- **Risk management is the holy grail — define and accept the loss before entering; it never
  changes even as entry tactics do.** Entry style (breakouts vs pullbacks) rotates with the
  market; capping the downside and keeping losses small is the one constant that keeps you in
  the game long enough to learn. Place the stop before entry *even when bending other rules* —
  the stop order goes in immediately after the fill (never sit in a position without a working
  stop), it marks the level that proves the trade wrong, and it never gets widened after entry;
  trying to outsmart stops occasionally helps but loses over time — trust the stop rather than
  overriding it with a discretionary read in the moment. A stock losing its rising 9/21 EMA is a
  hard red flag that the setup is invalidated. The goal of a speculator, in his words, is to
  "suck less" — protect the downside first so you buy yourself time to develop the upside; that
  cuts the other way too: fear of taking the loss shouldn't cause you to skip a good opportunity —
  take the trade while the invalidation level is still holding, and if it fails, accept it and
  re-evaluate ("no risk, no reward").

- **Anticipating a breakdown/breakout before confirmation = take less risk (e.g. half size).**
  Aggressive early entries before the chart confirms are lower-probability, so cut the
  per-trade risk to compensate. Tightening a stop *below* structure to chase smaller risk is a
  tradeoff — you get shaken out more easily. Buying off the lows below the EMAs, or using a
  non-LOD stop (the 5/15-minute breakout-candle low), is the same tradeoff: lower win rate for
  tighter risk — offset it with tiny risk (~0.1–0.15%) and a willingness to re-enter. Win rate
  decays smoothly with distance from the declining EMAs on the short side too — "the more
  extended, the farther away you are from the declining EMAs, the lower your win rate will be" —
  extension doesn't disqualify a trade, it just has to be paid for with a tighter stop. Fast-moving
  assets like crypto don't give you time to wait for full confirmation after a breakout is obvious
  — you have to anticipate the move and plan the trade before the prior swing high/base actually
  breaks, accepting the lower win rate that comes with entering before the candle even closes. The
  flip side of chasing tight risk: an unusually tight stop on an explosive opening-range move can
  cause you to miss the trade entirely if it never pulls back — a real tradeoff between risk
  control and catching fast movers. After any powerful/parabolic prior move, treat the next
  breakout setup with extra caution even if the pattern looks bullish — smaller size, tighter
  stops, followed strictly, weighed against how extended the broader market already is.

- **Never increase risk in a drawdown — reduce size; sizing up to win it back is a death
  spiral.** His most painful lessons (a 0.5% risk turned into a 35% drawdown; 2024 mistakes)
  all came from raising risk while losing. In a drawdown either you're trading badly or the
  market doesn't suit your style — smaller size is the answer to both. The urge to size up and
  win it back "as fast as possible" (his own instinct 2–3 years ago) is the first step of the
  vicious cycle — even after a single day's string of stop-outs, keep size flat and don't keep
  shorting every bounce regardless of the risk or the market. Using margin isn't itself the cause
  of a large drawdown — revenge trading (raising size after a loss) blows up accounts with or
  without leverage; the real skill is controlling the revenge-trade impulse, not simply avoiding
  margin.

## Market posture & regime

- **Read breadth, leadership and rotation to gauge market health.** Improving breadth = mediocre
  names upgrading to your leading watchlist, IWM/indices recovering, strength broadening beyond
  the Mag-7/tech. Deteriorating = more leaders losing the 9/21/50-day, shrinking leadership,
  defensive staples leading — a green-ish index with defensives and midcaps leading, tech beaten
  down, and names selling off hard into resistance after the open is a risk-off tape beneath the
  surface, consistent with staying short. Healthy participation (not just mega-caps) is what
  justifies getting aggressive long; conversely, avoid initiating new shorts on breadth alone even
  when one specific sector looks locally weak (rolling over from a declining 50 EMA) — improving
  overall breadth argues other areas will absorb that single sector's weakness. Track the
  sequential death of speculative groups as the thermometer — quantum, then nuclear, then power
  gens, then software: when the speculative money is gone across groups, real damage is done and a
  much deeper correction is on the table; that evening's review is the moment to close the long
  book proactively rather than waiting for individual stops. When breadth genuinely flips — leading
  and lagging counts roughly inverting inside a couple of weeks — that alone can justify staying
  defensive even in your specialty sector while other groups (defensive/energy/financials/biotech)
  quietly get relatively stronger; he still won't trade outside his specialty just because it's
  showing relative strength. When several (four-plus) names in one sector act well together on the
  same day *and* the sector ETF is holding/clustering above its own EMAs, that breadth-across-names
  is more reliable evidence of real sector strength than any single stock's chart — and that same
  alignment (many correlated names hitting the same technical level the same day) is itself a
  signal from the market, not just a pile of individually good-looking charts.
